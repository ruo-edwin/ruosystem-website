Ask a shop owner how the week went and you will usually hear: "Sales were good." Ask how much profit was made, how much stock is sitting unsold, or how much customers owe, and the answer is often a pause.

That pause is where money leaks. A shop can sell KSh 300,000 in a month and still struggle, because the profit is stuck on the shelf as slow stock, in the notebook as credit, or lost in unrecorded M-Pesa.

You do not need to be an accountant. You need to look at the same seven numbers, on the same day, every week. When one moves in the wrong direction, you notice in days, not months.

Pick one day (Monday morning works well) and one place to write the numbers. The habit matters more than the tool.
1

Total Sales

This is the number everyone already knows, but know it properly: everything the shop sold in the week, cash, M-Pesa and credit together.

Compare it with last week and the same week last month. A single week tells you little, but three weeks falling in a row tells you something is wrong: a competitor, a stockout, a slow checkout or a season change.

Watch for: sales going up while profit stays flat. That usually means you are selling more at thinner margins, or giving away discounts.

2

Gross Profit and Gross Margin

Gross profit is what is left after you subtract what the goods cost you from what you sold them for.

Gross profit = sales minus the buying cost of the items sold. If you sold KSh 100,000 of goods that cost you KSh 70,000, your gross profit is KSh 30,000 and your margin is 30%.

Margin matters more than sales because it shows how much of each shilling you actually keep. As a rough guide, cosmetic shops often work with higher margins than mini supermarkets, where staples like sugar, flour and cooking oil leave thin margins. Your own numbers matter more than any average.

Watch for: margin dropping week after week. Common causes are supplier price increases you did not pass on, wrong prices on the shelf and staff discounts nobody recorded.

3

Expenses and Net Profit

Gross profit is not what you take home. Rent, salaries, electricity, transport, airtime, licences, packaging and your own "small withdrawals" all come out of it.

Net profit = gross profit minus all expenses. Add up the week's expenses, including the small ones. Ten "just KSh 200" payments is KSh 2,000.

If you pay rent monthly, divide it by four and include a quarter of it each week, so the weekly number is honest.

Watch for: expenses growing faster than gross profit, and money taken from the till for personal use that never gets written down.

4

Stock Value

Stock value is the buying cost of everything on your shelves and in the store right now. It is your money, sitting in product form.

Too little and customers find empty shelves. Too much and your cash is locked up in boxes while you struggle to pay suppliers. The goal is to see the number move slowly and for a reason, such as a planned restock before Christmas, not by accident.

Counting everything every week is hard in a big shop. Start with your top 20 fastest sellers and your most expensive items, then work up to a full count each month.

Watch for: stock value rising while sales stay flat. You are buying more than you sell.

5

Slow Movers

Slow movers are products that have not sold in a long time, say 60 days or more. They are the quiet killers of small shops: they look like stock but behave like dead money.

Each week, list the items that have not moved and add up what you paid for them. Then decide what to do:

  • Bundle them with fast sellers ("buy this, get that at a lower price")
  • Discount them a little to free up cash, before they expire or go out of fashion
  • Stop reordering them until the shelf is clear

This matters most for cosmetics, where products expire and trends change, and for mini supermarkets, where food has a shelf life.

Watch for: slow movers making up a growing share of your total stock value.

6

Credit Owed

Credit keeps regular customers loyal, but unpaid credit is your money in someone else's pocket. Total up everything customers owe you at the end of the week.

The total is a start. Also ask who owes the most, who has owed the longest and whether the total is growing faster than your sales. A balance that is 5 days old is a reminder. One that is 60 days old is a problem.

Watch for: credit growing every week, a few customers owing far more than the rest and old balances nobody mentions anymore.

7

Cash and M-Pesa: Expected vs Counted

Take what your sales record says you should have received in cash and M-Pesa, then compare it with what you actually have: the cash in the till plus your M-Pesa till or paybill statement.

The difference is the number to watch. A small difference now and then is normal, for example from change or a rounding. A difference that keeps appearing means unrecorded sales, wrong change, mistakes or missing money.

Watch for: the same shortfall on the same days or the same cashier's shift, and M-Pesa payments that arrive but never appear in the sales record.

Frequently asked questions

What numbers should a shop owner check every week?

Total sales, gross profit and margin, expenses and net profit, stock value, slow-moving stock, credit owed, and the difference between expected and counted cash and M-Pesa.

How do I calculate gross profit for my shop?

Subtract what the items you sold cost you from what you sold them for. If you sold goods for KSh 100,000 that cost you KSh 70,000, your gross profit is KSh 30,000.

What is a slow-moving item?

A product that has not sold for a long time, often 60 days or more, depending on the shop. Cosmetics and food have a shelf life, so slow movers can quickly become losses.

How often should I count my stock?

Count your fastest sellers and most expensive items weekly, and do a full count monthly. A POS that updates stock with every sale makes this much easier.

Yes. It is a Excel file that opens in Google Sheets, Excel and most phone spreadsheet apps. The formulas for profit, margin and percentages calculate automatically.
