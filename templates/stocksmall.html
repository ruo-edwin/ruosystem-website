How to Track Stock and Sales in a Small Shop in Kenya

Learn how to record sales, monitor stock levels and keep accurate business records using simple tools or a POS system.

The easiest way to track stock and sales in a small shop is to record every sale, update stock whenever goods arrive, and regularly compare physical stock with your records. A POS system can automate much of this process, helping you reduce manual work and monitor your business more easily.

Whether you run a mini supermarket, cosmetics shop or general retail shop in Kenya, accurate stock and sales records help you know what is selling, what needs restocking and where discrepancies may exist.

01 — RECORD SALES

Record Every Sale Immediately

Every sale should be recorded as soon as it happens. If you sell products without updating your records, your stock figures and sales totals can quickly become inaccurate.

You can record sales using:

  • A notebook or manual sales book.
  • An Excel spreadsheet.
  • A POS system that records transactions.

For example, if you sell five bottles of lotion, your records should show the sale and the reduction in available stock. This makes it easier to compare what you sold with what remains on your shelves.

Simple rule: If a sale is not recorded, your business records may not reflect what actually happened.

02 — UPDATE STOCK

Update Stock Whenever You Receive Goods

Stock management is not only about recording sales. You must also record new products received from suppliers, damaged goods, returns and other stock movements.

Imagine your cosmetics shop has 20 bottles of lotion. You purchase another 30 bottles from your supplier. Your stock should now be 50 bottles, assuming there are no other stock movements.

If you later sell eight bottles, the expected balance becomes 42 bottles.

Expected closing stock = Opening stock + Stock received − Units sold − Other stock reductions.

Recording stock received and sold consistently helps you identify products that are running low before they run out completely.

03 — PHYSICAL CHECKS

Compare Your Records with Physical Stock

Even if your records look correct, count the actual products on your shelves and compare the quantities with your recorded stock.

For example, your records may show 25 units of a product while your physical count finds only 21. That four-unit difference needs investigation.

Possible reasons include:

  • Sales that were never recorded.
  • Damaged or expired products.
  • Stock movements that were not entered.
  • Counting or data-entry errors.
  • Missing goods or theft.

Investigate discrepancies and correct your records only after identifying or documenting the reason for the adjustment.

04 — CHOOSE YOUR TOOL

Should You Use Excel or a POS System?

Both Excel and POS software can help you track stock and sales. The right choice depends on the number of products you sell, your daily transactions and how much manual work your shop can handle.

When Excel is enough

Excel may be sufficient if you have a small number of products, few daily sales and time to maintain your records carefully.

When a POS system makes more sense

A POS system becomes more useful when you manage many products, make frequent sales or need reliable stock and sales reports without updating multiple spreadsheets manually.

Keep in mind that stock updates depend on correctly recording sales, purchases and other stock movements. A POS system cannot automatically account for goods it has not been told about.

05 — SALES REPORTS

Use Sales Reports to Make Better Decisions

Good stock records show what you have. Sales reports help you understand what is happening in your business.

Review your records to identify:

  • Products that sell quickly.
  • Products that have remained on the shelves.
  • Items that need restocking.
  • Your daily and monthly sales totals.
  • Differences between recorded and physical stock.

Remember that sales revenue is not the same as profit. To estimate gross profit, subtract the cost of the goods sold from the sales revenue. Other business expenses must also be considered when calculating overall profit.

06 — THE BOTTOM LINE

Make Stock and Sales Tracking Part of Your Routine

To track stock and sales in a small shop in Kenya, record every sale, update incoming stock, count physical inventory regularly and review your reports.

A notebook or Excel spreadsheet can work for a very small shop. As the business grows, a POS system can reduce repetitive work by connecting sales, inventory and reporting.

The goal is simple: know what you have, understand what you sell and use accurate records to make better business decisions.

Better stock records mean better visibility into your business. Choose a tracking method you can maintain consistently as your shop grows.
